Scenario-Based Life Cycle Screening of U.S. Shrimp Aquaculture
Energy Demand, Feed Efficiency, and Environmental Trade-offs

Lihong Yang, BSE 5984 Sustainable Agroecosystems
November 24, 2026

Abstract

This study examines feed and electricity demand in Indiana biofloc shrimp production and an Alabama pond counterfactual. Literature inventories and ecoinvent backgrounds were used to calculate 100-year global warming potential (GWP) per kilogram of live-weight shrimp. An inventory audit restored larval airfreight and Alabama fertilizer supply. Indiana's covered GWP increased to 2.577 kg CO₂-eq/kg, with feed contributing 72.48%. Varying feed conversion ratio (FCR) by ±25% changed this subtotal by ±18.12%, compared with ±4.35% for farm electricity. The Alabama counterfactual reached 5.613. Hatchery production and direct farm emissions remain unresolved. These are expanded inventory subtotals, not complete farm carbon footprints or evidence of a technological advantage.

1 Introduction

Feed formulation, electricity demand, and inventory coverage can all affect comparisons of shrimp production systems. Al Eissa et al. (2022) examined these factors across shrimp supply chains and supplied the foreground data used here. This study tests farm electricity demand, grid intensity, and FCR while holding feed supply constant. The functional unit is 1 kg live-weight shrimp at the farm gate. The intended boundary is cradle-to-farm-gate, but the available inventory supports only partial GWP screening. 1.1 Evidence from previous studies

Feed supply and electricity are established priorities in shrimp LCA. Cao et al. (2011) assessed Chinese intensive and semi-intensive production and identified both as major contributors to GWP. They also evaluated electricity-mix changes and lower FCR. Sun et al. (2023) compared Chinese recirculating aquaculture systems, biofloc technology, and higher-place ponds. Electricity was a major driver, and higher-place ponds had the highest GWP among their cases. Together, these studies show that the magnitude and ranking of impacts depend on operating inputs and background energy supply. Identifying feed and electricity hotspots or applying sensitivity analysis is therefore an established approach.

The study by Al Eissa et al. (2022) provides the closest precedent and the foreground inventory for this project. Their cradle-to-US-market assessment compared three production chains and eight feed formulations. Feed production was the main GWP contributor for the Indiana chain, while feed substitutions produced trade-offs across impact categories. Their comparison included differences in recipes, delivery distances, farming inputs, and post-harvest operations. It addressed the performance of those supply chains. The present farm-gate analysis asks how selected differences contribute when feed supply and the comparison boundary are held constant.

Wastewater studies show why climate results need a broader environmental context. Arbour et al. (2024) assessed microalgae treatment using the same Indiana farm background described by Al Eissa et al. (2022), so it is a related extension rather than independent farm evidence. The modeled treatment improved eutrophication outcomes without reducing GWP. Results also depended on electricity supply and the treatment of algal coproducts. These findings support assessing nutrient management alongside energy use, while keeping benefits outside this report’s GWP calculation explicit.

The studies differ in geography, system boundaries, feed backgrounds, and impact methods. Their absolute footprints cannot be compared directly with the covered subtotals reported here. This targeted narrative synthesis establishes the basis for the scenarios and their interpretation. It does not establish an exhaustive literature gap or priority claim.

1.2 Study objective and contribution

The study asks how much of the modeled Indiana–Alabama difference remains when both cases share Feed C, delivery distance, and a national electricity factor, and how that difference responds to FCR and grid intensity. The contribution is a reproducible, case-specific decomposition of published inventories. A fixed-FCR contrast isolates the electricity-demand effect within the model. Retaining each case’s literature FCR in a separate contrast exposes the feed-efficiency offset. Farm-only and farm-plus-mill grid scenarios then distinguish changes at different supply-chain stages.

The analysis also separates quantified inputs from unresolved burdens and evaluates when those gaps could overturn the covered-inventory ranking. The electricity-unit correction is internal quality control, not a new LCA method or a criticism of the source studies. This is an analytical extension using secondary data. It provides neither new farm measurements nor causal evidence that one farming technology performs better.

2 Materials and methods

2.1 Inventory and background data

Feed C is the estimated Indiana commercial-feed recipe reported by Al Eissa et al. (2022). The manufacturer did not disclose the full formulation. This is the only recipe modeled here, and both cases use it. Appendix A gives the farm inputs (Table A1), feed recipe (Table A2), and processing inventory (Table A3). Appendix C, Figure C1 maps the intended cradle-to-farm-gate boundary, inputs, product output, and unresolved flows.

Table 1

Selected literature inputs per kilogram of live-weight shrimp

Note. Inputs are from Al Eissa et al. (2022, Tables 1 and 2). Both inventories are secondary literature data.

The recipe contains 30% fishmeal, 5% poultry by-product meal, 20% soybean meal, 30% wheat flour, 5% rice bran, 3% fish oil, 3% soybean lecithin, and 4% supplements by mass (Al Eissa et al., 2022, Table 1).

Background datasets cover 83% of Feed C ingredient mass. The remaining 17% uses meat-and-bone meal for poultry meal, wheat bran for rice bran, refined soybean oil for lecithin, and Norwegian vitamin/mineral mix for supplements at 1.33 kg CO₂-eq/kg (Winther et al., 2020, p. 51). Feed processing and 1006 km delivery are included. The proxies are not fully harmonized. The meat-and-bone meal factor is especially low because of how its dataset treats a rendering by-product, whereas the source study used mass allocation (Al Eissa et al., 2022, p. 2009). Appendix A evaluates its effect.

2.2 Life cycle impact assessment

GWP was calculated with openLCA 2.7, Python 3.12, ecoinvent 3.11 Cut-off, and IPCC 2021 GWP100 excluding biogenic CO₂. Units and flow properties were specified (GreenDelta, n.d.). Covered GWP is G = F × C + E × g + B + L + Q. Here, F is FCR, C delivered feed GWP, E farm electricity, g the grid factor, B bicarbonate, L larval airfreight, and Q fertilizer supply. The original feed-and-electricity contrast is retained separately. Factors are in Table A4, and the completeness audit is in Appendix B.

2.3 Scenario design and consistency checks

One-at-a-time scenarios varied F, E, or g by ±25%. Further scenarios combined grid factors of 0.05 to 0.80 kg CO₂-eq/kWh with FCR values of 1 to 2. Alabama was evaluated at its literature FCR and at fixed FCR 1.50, with other inputs held constant. Checks covered returned reference demands, contribution sums, and baseline identities. These deterministic ranges are not confidence intervals. Appendix A, Table A5 lists the scenario settings.

2.4 Model assumptions

Both cases share Feed C, processing inputs, 1006 km delivery, and a national electricity proxy. Alabama retains its literature FCR. Performance on this recipe is unverified. Airfreight and fertilizer quantities use literature values and documented background proxies. Unknown farm burdens remain unresolved, not zero. Appendix B distinguishes restored inputs, external benchmarks, and data gaps.

3 Results and discussion

3.1 Indiana hotspots and model consistency

The U.S. electricity factor remains 0.487361 kg CO₂-eq/kWh and delivered Feed C GWP remains 1.245165 kg CO₂-eq/kg feed. Restoring larval airfreight adds 0.118 to Indiana's earlier 2.459 subtotal, giving 2.577. Figure 2 shows contributions to this expanded inventory. Unresolved burdens are not represented by zero-valued bars.



Note. Covered GWP is 2.577. Feed contributes 72.48%, electricity 17.42%, bicarbonate 5.53%, and larval airfreight 4.56%. Optional farm-water treatment is separate.

[Figure 2 — expanded_hotspots.png]

The electricity audit corrected a per-MJ result to an explicit 1 kWh demand (3.6 MJ). The 2023 U.S. benchmark of about 0.367 kg direct CO₂/kWh provides a scale check, but its generation boundary differs from delivered life-cycle GWP (U.S. Energy Information Administration, n.d.). New process-target calculations reproduced the reviewed electricity factor. Contribution sums reconciled and no database entities were written.

The optional 75 kg treated-water proxy raises the subtotal to 2.667. Hatchery production, direct farm GHGs, and wastewater fate still need suitable data. Published hatchery results provide an external sensitivity check, not a US replacement factor (Cao et al., 2011) and are assessed in Appendix B.

Feed dominance is consistent with the Indiana result of Al Eissa et al. (2022). Its contribution share is not directly comparable because this model uses different feed proxies and omits post-farm stages and unresolved burdens. The stronger electricity role in Sun et al. (2023) also cautions against transferring a hotspot ranking across farms without checking their inventories and electricity backgrounds.

3.2 Sensitivity to feed efficiency and electricity



Note. In panel a, farm electricity and farm-grid responses overlap. Panel b varies the farm grid alone or both farm and feed-mill grids. The black point marks the reviewed baseline.

[Figure 3 — expanded_sensitivity.png]

Varying FCR from 1.125 to 1.875 gives 2.110 to 3.044 kg CO₂-eq/kg shrimp, or ±18.12% of the expanded subtotal. A ±25% change in farm electricity or grid intensity changes that subtotal by ±4.35%. The electricity responses overlap through E × g. Larval transport is fixed in these scenarios. Survival, growth, and operating responses are not predicted, and unresolved emissions could change the percentage sensitivities.

Farm-only grid scenarios give 2.174 to 2.865 kg CO₂-eq/kg shrimp. Changing farm and mill grids gives 2.089 to 2.926. Ingredient backgrounds remain fixed. The second case assumes coordinated electricity changes across farm and mill. The updated joint grid/FCR surface is supplied separately. These deterministic ranges describe the covered inventory, not uncertainty bounds for the whole farm.

3.3 Controlled comparison with the Alabama inventory

Both cases used the same Feed C supply chain, 1006 km delivery, and national grid factor. Alabama retained its literature FCR as a counterfactual input. Performance on this recipe has not been verified. Holding FCR fixed isolated the effect of farm electricity demand (Figure 4).



Note. Both cases use the same modeled feed and exclude bicarbonate. The Alabama counterfactual does not reproduce its original production system.

[Figure 4 — alabama_comparison_clarified.png]

With feed supply and farm electricity included, GWP was 2.317 for Indiana and 5.558 kg CO₂-eq/kg shrimp for the Alabama counterfactual. Alabama reached 6.156 at fixed FCR 1.50. Higher electricity demand added 3.839, while the lower literature FCR offset 0.598. With the mill grid fixed, the literature-FCR ranking reversed near a shared farm-grid factor of 0.076. Increasing Alabama delivery to 1677 km raised its subtotal to 5.665.

3.4 Interpretation and limitations

With larval transport and fertilizer supply restored, the expanded subtotals are 2.577 for Indiana and 5.613 for Alabama (Appendix B). The fixed-FCR contrast above remains restricted to feed and electricity. Climate, management, scale, and data provenance still confound any technology comparison.

Direct N₂O production has been demonstrated in shrimp, but available laboratory evidence is not a US farm emission factor (Heisterkamp et al., 2016). Hatchery, infrastructure, and wastewater gaps are assessed in Appendix B. GWP alone cannot establish overall sustainability. Eutrophication, water scarcity, biodiversity, and economics require separate assessment.

4 Conclusions

Feed dominates Indiana’s covered inventory, while electricity dominates the Alabama counterfactual. The common-feed comparison separates modeled electricity and FCR effects. The completeness audit identifies missing evidence that must be resolved before these subtotals can support farming-technology decisions.
